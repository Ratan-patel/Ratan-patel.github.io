Read this before you use any generated document.
  • These are templates, not legal advice. Your client's legal team — and, where appropriate, your own — must review anything before it is signed.
  • An authorisation is only as strong as the person who signs it. It must be signed by someone with genuine authority over the assets, not by an engineer who "has permission from their manager".
  • Written authorisation does not transfer liability. Testing outside the agreed scope can still be a criminal offence and a civil claim, even with a signed letter in hand.
  • Testing a third-party cloud, SaaS or hosted service usually requires that provider's permission as well as your client's. A client cannot authorise someone else's infrastructure.
  • No document protects you if you test assets you were not asked to test. Scope discipline is a habit, not a form.

Scope

Ambiguous scope is the most common cause of a "friendly" test becoming an incident. Name hosts, ranges, paths, accounts and builds.

Silence is not a boundary. If it is not excluded in writing, an assessor can reasonably assume it is in scope — and that assumption is what gets people prosecuted.

Contacts & escalation

A blind test still needs a named person who can stop it. Blind for the SOC is fine; blind for the business is negligent.

Permitted activities

Only the two baseline activities start enabled. Everything high-impact starts switched off — enable it solely where the client has consciously agreed, in writing. High-impact selections change the generated ROE and raise readiness warnings.

AUTHORISATION TO TEST

What a proper Rules of Engagement contains

A one-page purchase order is not an ROE. These are the clauses that matter when something goes wrong at 2am and someone has to decide whether to keep going.

  1. Named assets, not categories

    "The web application" is not a scope. "https://app.acme.example, staging tenant, /v2 API paths only" is. Every asset should be identifiable by a stranger reading the document.

  2. Explicit exclusions

    Third-party and shared infrastructure, payment systems, medical or safety-critical systems, employee personal devices, and everything not in the in-scope list. An empty exclusions section is a red flag in any ROE review.

  3. Who can authorise, and who can stop

    One named signatory with authority over the assets, and one named person who can halt the engagement immediately, 24×7, including outside business hours.

  4. Time windows and blackout periods

    Explicit start and end, in a stated timezone. If the engagement has no end date, you have no way to prove a later finding was out of scope.

  5. Permitted techniques, individually consented

    Social engineering, physical testing, denial of service, persistence and data-exfiltration proof are each separately negotiated. Bundling them into "testing" is how engagements end up in court.

  6. Defined impact ceiling

    What happens if a test accidentally causes an outage, corrupts data, or locks an account. Who is called, in what order, and within what time. Agree this before it is needed, because you will not be thinking clearly when it is.

  7. Abort criteria and the abort path

    The specific conditions that stop testing immediately — a production outage, a real security incident in progress, a third-party system being touched accidentally — plus the out-of-band channel used to issue the abort. Not the engagement chat group.

  8. Blue-team coordination and detection handling

    Whether the defenders are informed, and what happens when they detect you and respond. A red team that gets blocked and says nothing produces a report that is wrong about the client's security.

  9. Data handling, retention and destruction

    What data you may access, where captures are stored, who can reach them, and the date they are destroyed with written confirmation. If personal data is involved, a data-processing agreement belongs here too.

  10. Liability, insurance and governing law

    Professional indemnity cover, the limits of the assessor's responsibility, and the jurisdiction that governs the contract. Have this reviewed — it is the clause that gets tested in court, not the technical annex.

The test to apply to your own ROE: hand it to an engineer who has never met the client, at 2am, and ask them whether they can tell exactly what they may and may not do. If they have to guess, the document is not finished.

Legal basis — why the paperwork matters

The technical skills are what get you hired. Knowing where the legal line sits is what lets you keep working.

INDIA

Information Technology Act, 2000

Unauthorised access, downloading or manipulation of data in a computer resource is addressed under §43 (civil liability), §66 (computer-related offences) and related provisions, with aggravated penalties where critical information infrastructure or protected systems are involved. "I was doing security testing" is not a defence — written authorisation from the asset owner is.

This is a plain-language summary for assessors, not legal advice. Consult a qualified lawyer for your jurisdiction and engagement.

COMPARATIVE

Other jurisdictions

Frameworks such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the UK Computer Misuse Act, and equivalent computer-misuse statutes in most countries criminalise access beyond what is authorised. Some jurisdictions add mandatory reporting duties if you encounter evidence of a separate crime, including certain categories of illegal content.

If you operate across borders, the ROE should name the governing law and confirm where evidence is permitted to be stored.

CLOUD & THIRD PARTIES

You cannot authorise someone else's systems

A client's signature does not extend to infrastructure they do not own. Testing cloud-hosted infrastructure, shared SaaS, or a payment provider typically requires that provider's own permission under their policy. Testing a shared platform's controls on behalf of one tenant can affect every other tenant — a fact that turns a procedural slip into a serious incident.

Read the provider's penetration-testing policy before you touch anything hosted by them, and note the policy version in your engagement reference.

PROOF

What actually protects you

A signed letter from someone with authority, describing named assets and a date range, stored somewhere you can retrieve it quickly. Not a screenshot of a chat message from a colleague. Not an email that says "go ahead" without naming what "ahead" means.

If you cannot produce the authorisation within minutes of being asked, assume you cannot prove you had it.

A practical habit: before touching anything, put the authorisation letter, the ROE, the emergency contact list and the abort procedure in one folder on the machine you will operate from. Offline copy as well. The moment you need them is a moment you will not have time to search for them.

How engagements go wrong

Nearly every incident traceable to process — rather than skill — comes from one of these six.

01

Starting before signing

"The contract is with legal, we can begin the recon." Recon is testing. Nothing begins before the signature, including passive work, because the authorisation clock has to cover the whole engagement.

02

Category scope

"All company web applications." Which environment? Which tenant? Whose third-party integration? Scope by category is scope by assumption.

03

Assuming exclusions are obvious

If the exclusions section is blank because "obviously we wouldn't test payroll", that thought belongs on paper. Reviewers, insurers and lawyers do not share your instincts.

04

No agreed impact ceiling

An account lockout, a test record in production, a triggered failover at 3am. Without a pre-agreed response, the conversation stops being about security and starts being about blame.

05

Blind for the wrong people

Hiding the test from the SOC is a legitimate way to measure detection. Hiding it from the person who can stop it, or from the on-call engineer who will be paged at 2am, is how a test becomes an incident.

06

Evidence kept forever, everywhere

Credentials, screenshots of personal data and memory dumps left on a laptop for eighteen months. Retention and destruction dates are not administrative overhead; they are the part of the engagement a regulator will ask about.
